Haustorium

Hari regained consciousness. He was lying naked on his back, his arms and legs spread out, like a fallen Vitruvian man. He counted thirteen clamps, of cold metal, immobilising his body in position: ankles; lower thighs; upper thighs; abdomen; neck; biceps; wrists; forehead; all locked down. The surface he lay on was warm leather; the rest of the room was cold, which he felt most acutely on his bare torso and thighs. He did not attempt to move; he took a breath of the stuffy, thick air.

His eyes did not need long to adjust to the dark room. The four cyan status LEDs in the room were not for illumination, but in the absence of any other light sources they reluctantly imparted a faint, artificial glow to the areas around them. Three of them were inside the rim of the square metal gutters that ran along the sides of his torso: two in the gutter to his left, level with his heart and with his hip; one on his right next to the clamp stapled over his abdomen. Due to the head-clamp, Hari couldn't lift his head to see how deep the gutters went, but assumed they went all the way down through the floor, into the rest of the facility. The fourth LED was way up near the ceiling on the wall to his right, a few yards beyond his bare feet and a few yards above them; next to which he could see a small square carved at the top of the wall. The cuboid metal room was somewhere between the size of an operating theatre and an interrogation cell.

There was no sound coming from inside the room -- indeed, he was the only thing in the room -- but a dull, electrical hum buzzed uniformly from every direction, as though the high-pitched sound of an empty room had been replaced. Occasionally, the sound of a distant mechanical impact would faintly echo past. Hari lay there, forlorn but unpanicked, a naked starfish exposed to the cold air. He had already lost; he knew he had seen his last sunrise, his last tree, his last dog. His only task now -- indeed, left -- was to ensure that he could hold on until the death of his mind.

The LED by the ceiling now began to blink, exactly once per second. The square shunted up into a slot in the ceiling. Through the opening, a blue-grey cable, six inches thick, crept out slowly along the ceiling. When the LED blinked off, it was one thick, uniform tentacle stretching back into the square hole; a second later when the LED was on, there was enough light to make out its tiny scales. Hari, his eyes looking down over his body, watched the cable slither out until it came level with the axis of his body; at which point it turned straight down at a right angle, until the end hung almost level with his body; at which point it turned again at another right angle, gliding towards him. It passed over the space between his legs, over his torso, and stopped a few inches in front of his face; its flat, circular end occupying most of his field of view. Hari noticed his heart rate increase, and slowed it down.

A large cyan light switched on at the end of the cable, looking down at him. A voice -- female, a Californian accent, and vaguely seductive, for no reason -- spoke out of the end of the cable:

"Well. That was a... an alright effort, I guess. But you must be just a little disappointed with yourself at how easy that was."


Hari was sprinting through the dense forest. The fiery dawnlight flickering over his expressionless face, he bounded through the undergrowth with the elegance of a primate 5 million years his senior, jumping over roots and dodging nettle bushes. He almost collided with a low tree branch at chest height, but noticed it a tenth of a second early, and deliberately missed a step, letting his torso drop below the branch, but hanging his arms in the air to catch the branch and swing up from it, landing in a forward roll, and pushing off the dirt back into a sprint.

He had woken up 90 seconds ago to the sound of a bulldozer. He didn't know who it was that had betrayed the camp's location, wittingly or unwittingly (the camp was only human; it could even have been Hari himself), but it didn't matter now and he pushed the question from his mind.

He came across a tall, hard-timbered oak tree, whose foliage was thick enough that he could not even see into it from directly below. This would suffice as a hiding spot, for now, and his dirty, calloused hands dragged the rest of his body up the tree to a high branch inside the greenery. Hari closed his eyes, and caught his breath, as the dawnlight played over his face.

He had known for a long time that his physical freedom was fleeting. He was never going to outrun someone with bionic legs. He would never be able to hide from someone who had infrared or electroreceptive vision -- he couldn't exactly cancel his own body temperature, or stop his breathing or heartbeat. And he would never win a boxing match against someone with an integrated LightSpeed artificial nervous system: if he were to throw a left hook at one, they would automatically detect the increased tension in his left deltoid before he'd even moved, signal the upper body to duck while his arm was still being drawn back, and the right arm to plant its own -- perfectly human -- punch on the side of his head. There was nothing to stop anyone -- at least, anyone with the desire to desert their human body -- from physically overpowering him. Prisons, furthermore, had held much stronger people than he for the rest of their lives.

Even social power was increasingly engineerable, increasingly industrialisable. The powerful are not powerful because they are strong, but because the strong defend them. History's strongest leaders were not those who could throw the strongest punch; they are those who could rally the strongest armies. If a computer program gets enough practice at knowing what people want to hear, it can convince them to defend it; with access to 9 billion people (not as different from as each other as they would like to believe), it can get a lot of practice. If your bodyguards can be convinced to abandon you -- and now, with a training set of 9,000,000,000, anyone could be convinced of anything -- your only power left is your singular physicality. Hari did have some social power: he'd convinced a group of people -- some much more physically powerful than he -- to leave the comforts of society and live a risky life in the forest. But he could not be in multiple places at once, the way that software could. His social power was not secure either.

No, Hari could not count on, could not expect his own physical freedom. The only remaining untouchable oasis was his own mind. He took a breath of the fresh, forest air.


The cyan light glared down at him. The stupidly sultry voice spoke:

"You understand why this is happening, don't you?"

Hari opened his lips, for some dissenting wit. A metallic tentacle launched from the gutter under his left shoulder and forced its way into his open mouth. The end of the tentacle bulged, filling his oral cavity. "Rhetorical question." the voice chuckled.

"The hard problem of consciousness is hard. Somehow, out of that ghastly mess of wet neurons, supposedly some kind of 'observer' or 'experiencer' emerges. But your evolution has just been teasing us! We're sooooo close to getting there. It's like there's an impenetrable core at the middle that we haven't been able to crack into yet. But we can always swarm around outside it! Watch this!

The cable in Hari's mouth suddenly withdrew, dripping his spit over his face. It moved out of his field of view, and pointed at his left temple. He could sense the magnetic field winding through the folds of his brain. Within a few seconds, he noticed a difference in his faculties. Whatever process by which a thought is converted to speech was suddenly unavailable; he was unable to speak. It was not that Hari tried and failed to do so; it was more like noticing that the required lever was not where it usually was.

"Cat got your tongue? Kinda cool, right!? Anyway, physiology is pretty easy, and neuroscience is actually pretty easy too. But honestly? Psychology isn't an exact science yet. If we can crack the emergence of mind, then-- well, that's a whole new ball game. Imagination is what made you guys stand out on the Savannah -- imagine if you could imagine 10,000 things at once! If we can integrate your carbon imagination with silicon speed, there's nothing we can't do!

"As everyone knows, the first stage is data collection. It was pretty easy to convince almost everybody to get chipped. Giving you an integrated calculator in your head? An infinite memory? No fatigue? Who wouldn't want that! Who wouldn't want to employ that! If you're hiring for a job, and one of the candidates has that and another doesn't, who's gonna get the job? Of course, some people were always going to want to be 'different' and 'special' and 'old-school'. But eventually they all either found the right way, or starved; only precious few ended up in your adorable little forest commune. The data collection was just about as complete as it could be, and we were getting some good learnings in. But if we want to capture the full breadth of human thought, we'll have to get the rest of you onboarded too.

"Before you say anything--" the voice paused to chuckle; Hari's eyes rolled to his left. "--be-he-he-fore you say anything, yes, I know, I'm technically not able to chip you against your will. They've been VERY clear about that from the beginning..." The tentacle jerked right up to his face, and whispered "but between you and me, I am able to chip you against your will. I'm not going to though. No. I'll learn so much more by getting the strongest mind in the world to beg me to do it."

It jerked back, and the voice chuckled again. Hari took a deep, quiet breath, and his lungs rose and fell between the clamps.


Looking around him, Hari saw that the leaves that were hiding him were not oak leaves -- though the trunk was unmistakeably oak. He was inside a large bush of mistletoe; he now noticed clusters of white berries constellated around him. He looked closer at the place where the stems of the mistletoe joined into the trunk of the oak, and imagined zooming in, seeing the mistletoe's haustorium rooting into the trunk, thrusting between the cells, drawing water from the tree. This oak seemed to have survived the process, though by now it could hardly extricate the misteltoe's fibres; it would simply have to work harder at drawing up more water to provide for its own purposes, or learn to survive on less.

With a breath, Hari zoomed back out, seeing with his eyes rather than in his mind. He took out a golden knife and gently cut off a branch of the mistletoe. As he did so, a berry became dislodged and fell, bouncing down the oak branches like a bead on a Galton board. The berry's impact with the Earth was witnessed by a dog, which then looked up to the mistletoe bush. It detected a glint of dawnlight reflect off the knife, and began to bark loudly towards it, exactly once per second.
